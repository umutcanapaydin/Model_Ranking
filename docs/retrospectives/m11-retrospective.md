# M11 Retrospective — the milestone that answered its own question and then met a user

## The carried question, answered

> **M10 asked: what is the smallest amount of RUNNING this project could add to its close, and what
> would it have to run? Not more tests. The two defects that mattered were found by running a
> suspect expression in isolation, and by putting the product in front of a person.**

**The answer is the second one, and it is not close.**

The tally is the argument. Across M11, execution of code found real defects — a `runner` section
calling commands that do not exist, a gate that passed on zero tests, a workflow that had never
once been green. Those are worth what they cost. But **a person using the app for twenty minutes
produced six defects that eleven milestones of gates had not caught**, and then produced five
product requirements that no one inside this project would ever have written.

The six: the on-device router could not decline, so every unmeasured question came back as a
confident measured match; the selected surface was not the one shown first; the preview repeated
the picks; the category strip reset on selection; the filtered list left the reader at an arbitrary
slice of a new list; and the strip left the chosen chip clipped at the edge.

None of those is a weak assertion. Every one of them is invisible to a test suite by nature —
they are about what a person concludes from what they can see. `make check` cannot hold an opinion
about whether "Agentic coding" answering a question about coding reads as a broken feature.

**So the smallest amount of running to add to a close is: one person, one session, and a written
list of what they asked and what came back.** The cost is an hour and the owner's attention. M11
paid it once and it returned six defects and a product direction. That is the recommendation, and
the honest caveat is that it is not a GATE — nothing mechanical can make it happen, and the only
thing that makes it repeatable is that the closure record has a row for it that cannot be filled
without a name and a date.

## What this milestone taught

### 1. A control in the path that does not execute is not a control

The router's three tiers were built with the disclosure — "this is not something we measure" — in
the SIMILARITY tier's floor. On any device carrying `FoundationModels`, that tier runs second and
therefore almost never runs at all. The model tier, which does run, was constrained by
`GenerationSchema(anyOf: known)` to the nine ids the engine serves and **had no expressible way to
decline**. Every question in the world came back as one of nine measured surfaces.

The requirement said "an unmeasured question routes to `assistant` and SAYS so". The code
satisfied it in a path nobody reaches. This project has recorded that shape four times — a control
cited but not run, a gate that cannot fail, a seam that does not reach the caller — and this is
its purest form yet, because the control was correct, tested, and unreachable.

### 2. Proving one half of a change proves nothing about the other half

`make swift-test` was written with a pipe, measured to return 0 with a broken assertion, and fixed,
with a comment recording the lesson. Four lines below it, the `runner` section shipped the same
class of defect by different mechanics: it called `pass` and `fail`, which are not commands in that
script. Both branches died silently and `runner` reported ALL PASS over a red suite.

The Makefile half had been proven by breaking a test and watching the gate go red. The runner half
was read. **The lesson did not transfer because it was learned as a fact about pipes rather than as
a habit of executing the thing you just wrote.** A third instance followed in the same wave — the
recipe passed on zero tests — which is what finally made it a habit.

### 3. The pile said twenty-two and it was seven

Twenty-two ledger rows sat in ESCALATED, meaning "waiting on the owner". Eleven had been resolved
by later work that never came back to close them; five described things that cannot be undone and
should have stopped asking. A pile that size does not read as eleven stale rows and seven live
ones — it reads as noise, and the owner could not tell which was which.

Every close in the audit was verified by RUNNING something rather than by reading the row. That is
the only reason it was trustworthy, and it is also why it had not been done before: reading
twenty-two rows is cheap and tells you nothing.

### 4. Prose in an API is a localisation trap, and it was already sprung

Asked for Turkish, the obvious reading was a string file. The measurement said otherwise: every
sentence on screen — titles, reasons, notices, disclosures — is generated by the engine in English.
The product had been building a localisation debt for eleven milestones without anyone naming it,
because there was no second language to reveal it.

The owner chose the architecturally right answer: the engine returns facts, the client composes the
sentence. It costs a contract move. The cheaper option would have written every future sentence
twice and paid the whole cost again on the third language.

## What went well

- **D-133 paid for itself inside the wave that created it.** The first independent seat returned
  BLOCKING on the machinery built to enforce K.7, and all three findings were real — including one
  where two absences cancelled into a green gate: `record_type: review` was missing from the schema
  AND `docs/reviews/` was missing from the manifest, so 44 files carrying an illegal type were
  never scanned.
- **The gate found the one record in the project claiming K.7 was satisfied and failed it**, on its
  first run, because that record cited two review files that have never existed.
- **`contract-tests.yml` is green for the first time in its life.** It had run twice and failed
  both times, and not for source drift: it was structurally unpassable, and because step 1 failed,
  the three steps below it had never executed once.

## What did not

- **A signed plan promise never became a requirement row.** REQ-RUN-002 — two unattended refresh
  cycles — was assigned to W3 and W3 closed without it, because it was never written into the PRD
  at its wave. Found by a closing audit that counted instead of recalling (W-071). Second time this
  project has quietly not delivered a plan item.
- **K.7 was still waived at W4's own review round in spirit**: the seat is a separate session, not
  a separate person, and D-133 says in its own text that it cannot prove independence.
- **Nothing deployed, fourth milestone.** D-123, W-030, W-031.
- **The design pass was handed over with half the screen unseen** and had to be re-verified after
  the owner granted a permission (W-066). Twice in one milestone I handed over work I had only
  read.

## The question carried to M12

> Eleven milestones specified this product. Every requirement in the PRD before 2026-08-23 was
> written by someone who already knew what a benchmark was, and the product they built explains
> what it measured in the language of the measurement — `161.7 ECI`, `$1.03/1M`, `Abstract
> reasoning`. It is all correct and a 60-year-old CFO cannot use any of it.
>
> The fix for each item is easy and obvious once stated. **The question is why none of them was
> ever stated**, and the answer is not that the team was careless — it is that a correctness
> culture measures whether a number is right and has no instrument at all for whether it is
> understood.
>
> So: **what would this project have to measure to catch the next one of these before a user
> does?** Not a gate that checks wording. Something that makes "would a stranger understand this"
> a question with an answer, in a repository whose entire discipline is built on answers that can
> be executed.
